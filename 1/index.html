Images of the
Russian Empire

Yong Tze Fon Timothy

Project 1 · Colourising the Prokudin-Gorskii photo collection · CS180: Intro to Computer Vision & Computational Photography · UC Berkeley · Fall 2026

Assignment brief

Sergei Prokudin-Gorskii photographed the Russian Empire in colour between 1907 and 1915 by exposing each scene three times onto one glass plate, through a blue, a green and a red filter. The Library of Congress scanned the plates. Each scan is one tall grey image with the three exposures stacked vertically.

This page reconstructs the colour photographs. I cut each scan into thirds, find the translation that aligns the green and red frames to the blue one, and stack them. I then add automatic cropping, contrast, white balance and a colour correction. I kept two methods that failed initially to explain the final choices.

01

The plates

A scan has three frames, top to bottom blue, green and red, each surrounded by its own border of unexposed and masked glass. The exposures were made in sequence with one camera, so the frames differ by a small translation, plus whatever moved in the scene. I read the scan as a grey image, cut it into three equal heights, and search for the (row, column) shift that moves G onto B and the one that moves R onto B.

The data comes as three small JPEGs (about 340 by 390 pixels per frame) and eleven 16-bit TIFFs (about 3200 by 3700).

02

Single-scale alignment

For the small scans, exhaustive search is enough. I try every shift within ±15 pixels in each direction (961 candidates), roll the moving channel by each one, and keep the shift whose result matches the reference best. I considered both normalised cross-correlation, the dot product of the two images after each has its mean subtracted and is divided by its norm, and L2 distance. In the end, NCC was used for all images.

\[ \mathrm{NCC}(a, b) = \frac{\langle\, a - \bar a,\; b - \bar b \,\rangle}{\lVert a - \bar a \rVert \; \lVert b - \bar b \rVert} \qquad\qquad \mathrm{L2}(a, b) = \lVert a - b \rVert_2 \]

NCC ignores an overall brightness difference between the channels; the L2 distance does not. On these plates the two metrics return identical offsets on 16 of 17 (the exception is in Part 06), so NCC is used throughout.

Scoring the whole frame gives wrong answers

Scored on the full image, the search returns wrong offsets on two of the three small plates. Scored only on the interior, it returns the right ones:

PlateG, whole frameG, interiorR, whole frameR, interior

Monastery(−6, 0)(−3, 2)(9, 1)(3, 2)
Tobolsk(3, 2)(3, 3)(6, 3)(6, 3)

Shifts are (rows down, columns right). The interior is the image with 34 pixels removed from each side.

The cause is the plate border. On the monastery's blue channel the edges are white on three sides (mean 1.00) and black on the fourth (0.08). The outer 34 pixels are 34% of the pixels but hold 58% of the mean-subtracted energy that NCC sums over. The border belongs to the glass, not the scene, so it sits in the same place in every channel and rewards a shift of zero. For the monastery's red channel, whole-frame NCC scores the wrong shift (9, 1) at 0.579 and the right one (3, 2) at 0.522; on the interior the same two shifts score 0.595 and 0.793.

The fix is to crop both images before scoring. The crop must exceed the largest shift tried, because np.roll wraps pixels across the edge and the outer d rows of a shifted image are garbage. It must also clear the physical border, which is about 20 pixels deep on the monastery. I crop 10% of the shorter side, or the window plus one, whichever is larger.

03

Image pyramid

At full resolution one candidate shift costs about 24 ms (a roll and an NCC over twelve million pixels). The ±15 window would take 23 seconds per channel and still not reach the shifts in the large scans, which run to 178 pixels. Covering ±180 exhaustively would take about 52 minutes per channel.

A pyramid does it in under a second. I halve both images, with area averaging so the small versions are not aliased, until the shorter side is at most 128 pixels; for the Emir that is five halvings, 3209 → 100 rows. At the coarsest level I run the full ±15 search, which covers ±480 pixels at full size. Then at each finer level I double the estimate from below and refine it with a ±2 search around that guess. The crop from Part 02 grows with the estimate, so wrapped pixels never enter the score. On a synthetic test the recursion recovers a known shift of (57, −33) exactly.

04

Aligning on edges

The first pyramid run aligned 13 of the 14 plates. The Emir's red channel came out at (401, −483). Dividing by 32, the scale of the coarsest level, gives (12.5, −15.1): the coarse search stopped at the corner of its ±15 window, which means it found no peak at all. This is because Emir's robe is bright in the blue exposure and dark in the red one, so the raw intensities of those two channels do not correlate.

To address this, I observed that edges do not depend on which side of them is brighter. As such, I align the gradient magnitude of each channel, computed with a Sobel filter, and apply the shift it finds to the original channel. The Emir's red channel moves to (107, 40).

Grain

Plain Sobel on the raw scan fixed the Emir but made the religious painting worse: its offsets moved from (28, 3) and (68, 7) to (30, 7) and (70, 6), and the figures grew doubled outlines. Each exposure has its own film grain, and a 3×3 Sobel amplifies that noise more than it amplifies real edges. To address this, I used a Gaussian blur of σ = 2 before the gradient which removes the grain and keeps the edges:

07

Automatic cropping

An aligned image has two kinds of unwanted edge. Rows and columns that np.roll wrapped around are known exactly from the shifts and are removed first. The plate border has to be detected.

Row statistics do not separate border from sky

A first rule called a row "border" if at least half its pixels were saturated in some channel, or if the three channels disagreed strongly along it. It kept 40% of the harvesters and cut 687 rows from the top of the church. A blue sky is a row where the channels disagree: the church's sky averages (0.51, 0.58, 0.88) in RGB for three hundred rows. A bright sky is nearly saturated: 48% of each harvesters row is above 0.95 in green, two points under the cutoff.

The edge of the border does

A plate border ends at a straight, sharp edge; a sky does not. For each channel and each side, I take the mean intensity of every line over the middle 60% of the other axis, and cut at the innermost place within the outer 10% of the image where that profile jumps by more than 0.1 across six lines. Frame edges jump by 0.4 to 0.99; picture content almost never exceeds 0.1. The tightest cut over the three channels is used. On the Emir's left edge the profile passes a white frame, a colour fringe and a dark unexposed band before the carved door, and the cut lands at column 219. Across the fourteen course plates the rule keeps 79% to 86% of each image.

The rule misses a border that fades out instead of stopping. On the self portrait's right edge the blue channel falls from 0.25 to 0.05 over forty columns, about 0.03 per six lines, and a faint strip survives. Lowering the threshold to 0.08 catches it but cuts into the icon and the three generations, so the threshold stays.

08

Contrast and white balance

Contrast. The scans are flat. I stretch the intensities so the 0.5th percentile of all three channels maps to 0 and the 99.5th to 1. Percentiles rather than the minimum and maximum, so a dust speck does not set the range; one stretch for all channels rather than one per channel, so the colour balance is unchanged. This runs last in the pipeline.

White balance. I estimate the colour of the light, then apply a gain per channel that makes that colour neutral. Gray world estimates the light as the mean of each channel; white patch estimates it as the 99th percentile. Gray world fails whenever the scene is not grey on average, which is common here: a plate of lake and sky is pushed toward the complement of blue, so the church's sky turns yellow-green. White patch handles it, and it also gives a better fit for the colour matrix in Part 09, so it is the default.

09

Colour mapping

Prokudin-Gorskii's filters were not the primaries of a modern display, so stacking the exposures as RGB leaves the hues slightly off even after white balance, and a per-channel gain cannot fix a hue. The correction is a 3×3 matrix applied to every pixel. There is no ground truth to fit it to, so I use sixteen patches on eight plates whose colour can be guessed: plaster walls, black coats, white blouses and pavements should be neutral; grass and leaves green; sky blue; melons orange; lilacs lilac. Each target keeps the patch's own luminance and only changes its hue, so the matrix corrects colour, not exposure. The patches are sampled from the cropped, white-patch balanced images, the point in the pipeline where the matrix is applied.

Fig. 16 · The sixteen reference patches

The least-squares solution mixes the channels more than expected:

\[ M = \begin{bmatrix} \phantom{-}1.144 & -0.464 & \phantom{-}0.230 \\ \phantom{-}0.040 & \phantom{-}1.054 & -0.058 \\ -0.585 & \phantom{-}0.940 & \phantom{-}0.694 \end{bmatrix} \]

The corrected blue is blue plus most of the green minus over half the red, which says the blue exposure recorded much of what a display calls green. Nine parameters fitted to sixteen points can overfit, so the number that matters is the held-out one. The mean RGB distance from the patches to their targets is 0.103 with no correction and 0.070 after the fit; refitting with each patch held out in turn and scoring the held-out patch gives 0.085. The matrix helps, and less than the training number suggests. Plates with no patch in the fit show no artefacts, though skies lean slightly cyan.

10

Limitations and final results

The crop leaves a thin strip where a border fades gradually, and cannot follow the ragged emulsion damage on the greenhouse and seed plates. Gray world remains an option and remains wrong on any plate dominated by one colour. The colour matrix leans skies toward cyan, and its off-diagonal terms are larger than sixteen patches can justify; more patches or a fit regularised toward the identity would help, and both need better target colours than guesses. The alignment itself is sharp on all seventeen plates; the next step would be to search over a small rotation and scale as well.
